# The True Cost of Cars

Car ownership looks cheap on the surface. It isn't. This page builds out the full cost — personal, public, health, and climate — and compares it to realistic car-free or car-light alternatives.
{: .fs-6 .fw-300 }

---

## Personal Cost: Owning vs. Alternatives

### What a car actually costs per year

The AAA $12K/year figure is for a new financed car — it skews high. A more honest breakdown:

| Cost | Older paid-off car | Average new car |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance | $1,500–2,500 | $2,000–3,000 |
| Gas | $1,500–2,500 | $1,500–2,500 |
| Maintenance/repairs | $500–1,500 | $500–1,000 |
| Registration/fees | $200–400 | $200–400 |
| Depreciation/payments | $0 | $4,000–8,000 |
| **Total** | **~$4,000–7,000** | **~$8,000–14,000** |

A realistic Carmel Valley two-car household (one newer commuter car, one older paid-off car): **$10,000–15,000/year combined.**

### NYC car-free comparison (personal case study)

- Subway: $133/month × 12 = **$1,596/year**
- Car rentals for weekend trips (3-4x/year, split with friends): ~**$300–400/year**
- **Total: ~$2,000/year**

Gap vs. one suburban car: **$2,000–5,000/year**
Gap vs. two suburban cars: **$8,000–13,000/year**

That gap is $650–1,100/month in additional housing budget that a car-free NYC resident has available — which reframes the "NYC rent is insane" argument considerably.

### The Uber/Zipcar alternative

A funny but legitimate comparison: what if instead of owning a car, you Ubered to work and rented a Zipcar on weekends?

- Uber commute (e.g. $20 each way, 20 days/month, split with one coworker): $400/month = **$4,800/year**
- Monthly transit pass for days you don't Uber: ~$50/month = **$600/year**
- Zipcar weekends (2x/month at $15/hour for 4 hours, split): ~**$720/year**
- **Total: ~$6,100/year**

vs. owning a car at $5,000–7,000/year minimum. You'd likely come out ahead, with no maintenance headaches, no insurance stress, no depreciation. The math only flips for people with very long, transit-poor commutes or who drive constantly on weekends.

*The car sits parked ~95% of the time. You're paying $5–12K/year for something that moves you around 5% of the time.*

## The Regressive Burden of Car Dependency

Car ownership costs don't scale with income — insurance, registration, and depreciation are roughly fixed regardless of what you earn. That makes mandatory car ownership a regressive tax:

- A household earning $35K spending $6K/year on a car is spending **17% of gross income** on transportation
- A household earning $120K spending the same $6K is spending **5%**
- In car-dependent areas, both households have essentially no choice — the car is mandatory

This is compounded by the fact that lower-income households are more likely to own older, less reliable cars — higher maintenance costs, worse gas mileage, more frequent breakdowns that cost jobs.

## Road Subsidies: Who Actually Pays for Roads?

Gas tax covers only a portion of road construction and maintenance costs. The rest comes from general taxes — paid by everyone, including non-drivers.

- Federal gas tax: 18.4 cents/gallon (hasn't been raised since 1993, not indexed to inflation)
- State gas tax (California): ~54 cents/gallon
- Combined, gas taxes fund roughly **50–70%** of road spending — the gap is covered by general funds

**The nuance worth holding:** Roads aren't purely a subsidy to drivers. They enable:
- Freight delivery (every store, every Amazon package)
- Emergency services
- Uber/Lyft/taxis (which non-drivers use)
- Buses running on those same roads

So the argument isn't "drivers should pay 100% of road costs." It's more: **drivers are subsidized at the margin, and that subsidy tips the scales toward car dependency in a way that has downstream costs.** If gas were priced to reflect its full infrastructure cost (plus externalities like pollution and congestion), driving would be somewhat more expensive and alternatives somewhat more competitive.

*Open question: What's the actual per-mile subsidy to drivers from general funds? Strong Towns has done some of this math — worth finding a cite.*

## Climate & Environmental Impact

*To be developed — needs its own deep dive.*

High-level: transportation is the largest source of US greenhouse gas emissions (~29%), and passenger vehicles are the largest share of that. But the climate argument is more interesting when you get specific:

- Per-mile emissions: car vs. bus vs. subway vs. walking
- The land use connection: sprawl forces more miles driven, multiplying emissions
- Urban heat island effect from asphalt (parking lots, roads absorb and re-emit heat)
- Air quality impacts concentrated in low-income areas near highways
- The EV counterargument: does electrification solve the problem, or just the tailpipe emissions piece?

*This deserves its own page eventually — "Climate Cost of Car Dependency" — connecting urban form, VMT (vehicle miles traveled), and emissions.*

## Health Costs

*To be developed.*

- Car-dependent environments correlate with lower incidental physical activity — you don't walk to the subway, you walk from the driveway to the door
- Epidemiological literature connecting sprawl to higher obesity rates, cardiovascular disease
- Air quality: particulate matter from traffic concentrated near highways, disproportionately affecting low-income neighborhoods built near freeways
- Traffic fatalities: ~40,000/year in the US — cars are one of the leading causes of death for people under 40
- Mental health: commute length and stress; studies showing long car commutes correlate with lower life satisfaction more than almost any other factor

*Good starting point: "The Built Environment and Physical Activity" literature; also Robert Putnam's work in Bowling Alone on commute time and social capital erosion.*

## The Prisoners' Dilemma of Car Dependency

Each individual rational choice (buy a car because transit is inconvenient) makes the collective outcome worse (transit becomes less viable, sprawl increases, everyone needs a car). No single person defecting from car ownership changes the equilibrium.

Cities that built transit infrastructure first ended up in a fundamentally different equilibrium — Tokyo, NYC, Amsterdam, Zurich. Once you're in the car-dependent equilibrium, escaping it requires coordinated infrastructure investment, not individual choice.

This is why "just take the bus" is not an answer in Carmel Valley. The bus doesn't go where you need to go, doesn't come often enough, and takes 3x as long. The individual is rational to drive. The system is irrational in aggregate.

Related: **Downs-Thomson paradox** — in equilibrium, the speed of car traffic matches the door-to-door speed of transit. If transit gets worse, driving gets worse too (more cars on the road). If transit improves, some drivers switch, roads clear, and the equilibrium restores. The implication: you can't build your way out of congestion with roads alone.
