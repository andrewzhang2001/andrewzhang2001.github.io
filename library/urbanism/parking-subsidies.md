# Parking Subsidies

The hidden cost of "free" parking and how minimum parking requirements shape cities against the people who live in them.
{: .fs-6 .fw-300 }

---

## The Core Claim

Parking is never free — someone always pays for it. Minimum parking requirements force developers (and by extension tenants, shoppers, and neighbors) to subsidize car storage, distorting land use in ways that make cities more expensive, less walkable, and less financially productive.

---

## The Mechanisms

*How does this actually work? Dump arguments here.*

- **Minimum parking requirements (MPRs):** Zoning codes mandate a certain number of parking spaces per unit or per square foot of building. These aren't market-driven — they're arbitrary numbers baked into law, often set decades ago for car-dependent suburbs and applied wholesale to dense urban neighborhoods.

- **The Shoup argument:** Donald Shoup's *The High Cost of Free Parking* is the foundational text. The core logic: when parking appears "free" to the user, its cost doesn't disappear — it gets bundled into rents, retail prices, and property taxes paid by everyone, including people who don't own cars.

- **Land opportunity cost:** Every surface lot or structured garage is land that isn't housing, isn't retail, isn't park space. In a high-demand city, that's not a neutral choice — it's an active subsidy to car storage over every other use.

## The Numbers

*What does this actually cost? Fill in data as you find it.*

- **Parking spaces aren't free to build** The cheapest 8.5 x 18 foot piece of asphalt on relatively worthless land costs $4000 to create. The most expensive parking space is an underground parking garage, which can cost $40,000+. Aboveground parking structures cost $20,000-$30,000. (*Walkable City*, 2012)

- **Ongoing maintenance costs** The ongoing costs of taxes, management, and maintenance costs about $125/month. This could theoretically be covered for. 50% parking occupancy from 9-5 charging $1/hour covers it. However, often times they don't. One study in the Mid-Atlantic determined the annual operating revenue per space recouped 26-36% of the annual cost.

- **The citizens subsidize parking** Cities subsidize parking. The cumulative subsidy was calculated in the 2000s to be $127-$374 billion. That's ~$1000/person, even for people like me that live in NYC and don't own a car.

- **Employer-paid parking subsidy** 90% of American commuters who drive to work pay nothing to park, often due to employer-provided spaces. The federal tax exclusion for these parking benefits cost taxpayers an estimated $7.3 billion annually. This encourages driving to work which is environmentally destructive, increase traffic congestion, and reduces tax revenue

- **Wasted parking spaces** There are an estimated 2 billion parking spaces in the US for just 250 million cars. A 2002 survey of Seattle's Central Business District found that at times of peak demand, almost 4 out of 10 parking spaces were empty. This occurs as a result of downtowns importing zoning standards form the suburbs

- **Parking cash-out** Businesses that offered the cash-out option saw their driving commuters drop by 11%

- Cost to build one parking space in a structured garage in NYC: ~$30,000–$50,000 (this gets passed directly into rent)
- Cost of a surface lot space: lower construction but enormous opportunity cost in land value
- Tax revenue per acre comparison: surface lot vs. mid-rise residential — see case study below

### Counterarguments

- **Businesses should be allowed to provide parking to lure customers**
  - Parking is not just allowed; it's required
  - Some cities insist parking is provided to visitors free of charge
  - Jeff Speck's counterargument: Imagine if cities required restaurants to offer a free dessert with each dinner. The price of dinner would increase to include the cost of dessert. In order to ensure restaurants didn't skimp, they'd have a minimum calorie requirement. Some diners would pay for desserts they didn't eat, others would eat desserts they wouldn't have ordered had they paid for them separately. Many people would get angry at even the thought of paying for desserts they had eaten for free for so long.
  - Alternative: businesses can pay fees that help finance parking spaces for their employees and customers in line with the value of the property located in a shared space. 
  - Subsidized parking doesn't behave according to the free market, it's a cost passed along to taxpayers

## Case Study: 350 E 52nd St, Manhattan (CB6)

A 142-unit residential building with an underground garage (~80 spaces) and ground-floor retail (Dominos). Data from ZoLa and PropertyShark, March 2026.

| | 142-unit residential building | Hypothetical surface lot (same land) |
|---|---|---|
| Lot size | 12,763 sq ft (0.29 acres) | same |
| Land value | $6.9M | $6.9M |
| Improvement value | $26M | ~$200K |
| Taxable assessed value | $13.8M | ~$3.2M |
| Annual property tax | $1.8M | ~$415K |
| Tax revenue per acre | **~$6.1M** | **~$1.4M** |

**The building generates 4.3x more tax revenue on the same land, served by the same city infrastructure.**

A few additional numbers worth sitting with:

- At $541/sq ft land value, each parking space (8.5 × 18 ft = 153 sq ft) sits on land worth **~$82,800**
- With 80 spaces, the garage consumes land worth **~$6.6M** — before factoring in $40,000+ per space to build underground
- Manhattan car ownership is ~22% citywide, likely lower in Midtown — meaning perhaps 28 of 142 units actually need parking. The building has 80 spaces, nearly 3x actual demand
- The 114 excess spaces represent construction and land costs passed into every tenant's rent, including the ~80% who don't own cars

**Note on underground parking and data invisibility:** Unlike surface lots, underground garages don't show up as a distinct land use in ZoLa or PLUTO — they appear as part of the building above. This means the subsidy is literally buried and harder to argue against politically, even though the cost is just as real.
