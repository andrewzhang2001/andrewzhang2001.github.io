# Plan Voisin

Le Corbusier's 1925 proposal to demolish most of central Paris north of the Seine and replace it with 18 uniform cruciform glass towers arranged on a strict grid, surrounded by parkland. He called the broader vision the **Radiant City** (*Ville Radieuse*).

![Scale model of Plan Voisin](/assets/images/urbanism/plan-voisin-model.jpg)
*Scale model of the Plan Voisin proposal*

## The Idea

The logic was essentially a vertical density trade-off: instead of spreading a dense population across low-rise blocks that filled every lot, concentrate everyone into towers and give the freed ground back as green space, light, and air. The towers would be spaced far apart, the streets below would disappear, and the city would become a series of parks punctuated by glass monoliths.

The plan was organized around **superblocks** — large consolidated parcels that eliminated the traditional street grid at ground level, freeing that space for parks, retail, and pedestrian walkways. These superblocks would be separated from each other by **superhighways** for automobile movement.

This came directly out of the context of the **1920s rise of the car**. The automobile was already threatening the Haussmann vision of the walkable Parisian boulevard — cars and pedestrians competing for the same street made the old model untenable. Corbusier's response was to separate the two entirely: cars on superhighways above or between superblocks, people on the ground level below. Rather than adapting the street to the car, he used the car as a justification to abolish the street altogether.

## Why It Was Criticized

- **Too rigid / inhuman** — the grid treated the city as an engineering problem, ignoring the street life, neighborhoods, and mixed-use grain that makes urban areas actually function
- Wiped out historic fabric entirely rather than working with it
- The "tower in the park" model isolates residents — parkland between towers tends to be dead space rather than activated public realm
- Prioritized cars and efficiency over pedestrians and community

## What It Influenced

Never built, but the ideas filtered heavily into post-war Western planning — particularly public housing projects in the US, UK, and France from the 1950s–70s. Many of those projects (Pruitt-Igoe in St. Louis, the Cabrini-Green towers in Chicago) eventually became symbols of failed urban policy and were demolished.

The tower-in-park typology persists in cities that built fast under modernist planning doctrine. Hong Kong's uniform residential towers in TST and Central have a visual kinship — though Hong Kong's version emerged from different pressures (extreme land scarcity, dense podium bases, active street-level retail) and functions far better as urban fabric.
