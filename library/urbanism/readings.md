## Companion to Urban Design

### The Open vs. Closed City (1800–1950)

Between 1800 and 1950, the **"open" paradigm** (exemplified by Plan Voisin) gained traction as a response to the overcrowding crisis in what were called **"whirlpool cities"** — dense metropolitan cores where mass migration had packed residents into conditions of polluted water, polluted air, and near-total absence of light, space, and ventilation.

The response was a movement to literally "open up" the city: towers spaced apart, ground returned to parkland, sun and air let in. (See [Plan Voisin](plan-voisin.md) for the clearest expression of this.)

But technological change shifted the dynamic. The **railroad and subway** allowed urban centers to decompress outward from their crowded cores, enabling a radical decentralization into low-density suburbs. Over time, automobile-dependent suburban development became the default mode of urbanism in the Western world.

The older values — **density, walkability, and spatial enclosure** — went from being the natural state of cities to being goals that require deliberate effort and creativity from urban designers to achieve.

### Haussmannization of Paris

The rebuilding of Paris under **Napoleon III and Baron Haussmann** in the mid-19th century is considered one of the most successful urban renewal projects in history. The core achievement was a network of wide boulevards and public spaces cut through the dense medieval fabric of Paris — at a time when movement across the city was becoming nearly impossible.

"Haussmannization" required the power and resources of an absolutist regime to demolish whatever stood in the way. The results justified it in the eyes of many:

- **High-capacity transportation** — the boulevards moved people across the city efficiently
- **Linear parks** — tree-lined rows created green corridors through the urban core
- **Vital public commercial space** — wide sidewalks, cafes, and retail activated the street continuously
- **Underground infrastructure** — sanitation and utility systems were built beneath the streets
- **Spatial enclosure** — uniform-height apartment buildings lined the boulevards, framing the street width; their many windows and narrow balconies gave the streets continuous life and animation
- **Terminated vistas** — streets were generally aligned to terminate at a monumental structure, giving the pedestrian a sense of destination and order

The Haussmann boulevard is essentially the opposite of the Plan Voisin model: rather than demolishing the city to create towers in parkland, it carved *through* the city to create structured, enclosed, mixed-use streets that remain among the most livable urban fabric in the world.
