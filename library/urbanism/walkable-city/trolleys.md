# Trolleys

Only large, fast transit systems have the potential to fundamentally transform cities — but that doesn't mean smaller systems can't be worthwhile. Smaller systems tend to fall into two categories:

- **Nodal** — connect several walkable districts together
- **Linear** — enhance and extend an existing walkable corridor

---

## Gondolas

The gondola connecting Telluride, Colorado to Mountain Village carries **2 million riders per year** at **one-fifth the cost per passenger** of local bus service. Gondolas are particularly effective at surmounting geographic obstacles like mountains, rivers, or highways that would otherwise require expensive tunneling or bridging.

## Streetcars / Trolleys / Trams

Streetcars are **pedestrian accelerators, not pedestrian creators**. They don't have a track record of filling empty sidewalks. If anything, the causality runs the other way — heavy existing pedestrian activity is what indicates potential for streetcar success. Build a streetcar into a dead corridor and it stays dead; build one into a busy corridor and it gets busier.

**When streetcars do make sense:** Portland showed that streetcars work best when a large area of vacant or underutilized land sits just beyond walking distance from a walkable downtown. The streetcar bridges that last-mile gap and spurs development in areas that would otherwise remain disconnected from the urban core — it's less about moving existing pedestrians and more about extending the reach of a walkable neighborhood into new territory.

**Funding model:** Private parties who stand to benefit from increased land value and connectivity along a streetcar line should want to help pay for it. In Seattle, Paul Allen contributed fully half the cost of a new $52 million trolley line connecting his property to downtown — the remainder came from federal/state sources and proceeds from selling surplus property along the route. This public-private model aligns incentives: developers get the transit-driven appreciation, and taxpayers don't foot the entire bill.

**Takeaway:** Trolleys, unless integrated into a robust citywide transit network, are first and foremost a tool for creating new urban districts — not a street-enlivening walkability bomb.
