# Dallas DART

In 1983, Dallas residents voted to levy a 1% sales tax to build the largest light-rail system in the US — DART now covers 72 miles of track. Despite continued expansion through the 2000s, overall transit ridership kept falling. There's been some development and land appreciation around DART stations, but it hasn't reduced highway congestion — the original goal.

---

## What Went Wrong

- **Insufficient residential density** — not enough people living close enough to stations to generate ridership
- **Abundant downtown parking** — driving remains the path of least resistance even in the urban core
- **Rail aligned for cost, not demand** — routes placed in cheap rights-of-way rather than where the most people actually are
- **Stations next to highways with giant parking garages** — optimized for park-and-ride, not walkable neighborhoods
- **Reduced frequencies to fund farther-flung service** — spreading the system thin made it less useful everywhere
- **No neighborhood structure at destinations** — you get off the train and you're not within walking distance of anything meaningful

**The core problem:** Park-and-ride only works if you don't need a car at both ends. Dallas suburbs lack the density and mixed-use fabric that would make the walk from the station to a destination possible. Transit without walkable urbanism just moves the parking problem from downtown to the station.

---

## The Takeaway

Investments in transit may be investments in mobility or real estate, but they are *not* investments in reduced traffic. The only way to reduce traffic is to reduce roads or to increase the cost of using them.

Civic leaders resist this because making driving inconvenient or expensive is politically toxic — nobody wants to be the one who took away fast, cheap driving. So transit gets funded as a feel-good alternative without anyone touching the conditions that make driving the default. The result is a system that grows and gets less useful at the same time.

---

## How to Fix It

**The ideal fix:** Congestion-price the freeways for maximum efficiency — making full use of the existing road investment — and use the revenue to bring transit fares to zero while shortening wait times. That's not going to happen.

**The realistic path forward:**

- Commit to focusing development around DART stations — create plans for walkable neighborhoods around every station, especially downtown, with pedestrian-friendly precincts and high housing densities within them
- Eliminate on-site parking requirements near all stations
- Prohibit new parking lots near stations
- ... and wait for gasoline to hit ten dollars a gallon (lol)
