# Transit

---

## Transit and Walking

When more than 25% of workers take transit, more than 10% commute on foot. When fewer than 5% take transit, fewer than 3% walk. This holds for non-transit users too — people in transit-oriented cities walk more regardless of whether they personally ride. Cities either organize themselves around driving or around everything else.

---

## Public Sentiment vs. Actual Funding

There's already broad public support for transit — the problem is that funding doesn't reflect it:

- A national poll funded by Transportation for America found the average respondent would allocate **41% of transportation funding to transit** vs. 37% for roads
- Respondents favored public transit over road building as a congestion solution by **3:1**
- Actual federal transportation funding allocates roads over transit **4:1**

The gap between what the public wants and what gets built is a political and institutional problem, not a demand problem.

### Case Study: Western Australia

Western Australia reversed a **5:1 roads-to-transit** spending ratio to **1:5 in favor of transit**. The investment funded a new rail system — ridership grew from **7 million to 50 million riders per year**. Demand for transit isn't the constraint; infrastructure is.

### The Net Savings Argument

Todd Litman compared the 50 largest US cities. In the 7 cities with high-quality transit service, each resident paid **$370 more per year** in public transportation costs than residents in the other 43 — but saved **$1,040 in vehicle costs**, a net gain of ~$670 per person per year. This excludes downstream savings from reduced pollution, healthcare, and traffic safety costs, which would widen the gap further.

## Case Study: Orange Line, Northern Virginia

The areas immediately around the five Orange Line Metro stops — less than **6% of the county's land area** — saw **70% population growth**. Today, **40% of residents in those blocks** use transit to get to work. Concentrated transit investment pulls density and ridership toward it; the two build on each other.
