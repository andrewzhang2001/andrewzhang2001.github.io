## Developer Economics: Why Don't They All Just Build High-Rises?

Came up during the 350 E 52nd case study. If a 142-unit building generates 4.3x more tax revenue and has a faster payback period than a surface lot, why doesn't every landowner just build vertically?

- **Construction financing risk** — you need to borrow $15-20M+ and service debt during construction before a single tenant moves in. A surface lot generates revenue from day one with near-zero construction cost. Some owners sit on lots as "lazy land banking" — low return, but also low risk, while they wait for the right moment.
- **Zoning constraints** — not every parcel is zoned for high density. Height limits, FAR (floor area ratio) caps, and use restrictions all limit what can be built, regardless of what the market would otherwise demand.
- **Market timing** — developers try to build when construction costs are low and rents/sale prices are high. Those windows don't always align.
- **Capital availability** — not every landowner has access to construction financing. A small landlord sitting on a surface lot in LIC may not have the balance sheet to get a $20M construction loan even if they wanted to.
- **Key metrics developers use:**
  - *Payback period* — how many years to recoup total investment
  - *Cap rate* — net operating income ÷ total asset value. Higher = better return. Dense residential beats parking decisively.
  - *IRR (internal rate of return)* — accounts for time value of money across the full investment horizon

**Open question:** At what land value per sq ft does it become irrational not to build vertically? There's probably a threshold where even risk-averse landowners can't justify leaving land as parking.

## Land Value Tax

Came up as a natural extension of the parking subsidy argument. Current property tax in NYC taxes both land and improvements (buildings) together. This creates a perverse incentive: building a better building raises your tax bill, so some owners undermaintain or underdevelop to keep assessments low.

**Land value tax (LVT)** — tax only the land, not the building. Same tax whether you build a 20-story tower or leave it as asphalt. Forces productive use because you owe the same regardless of what's on the lot.

- Henry George popularized this in *Progress and Poverty* (1879) — still has a devoted following in urban economics
- Applied partially in some Pennsylvania cities (Pittsburgh, Harrisburg) with mixed but generally positive results on development density
- Strong Towns advocates for versions of this as a fiscal reform

**Why it matters for parking:** A landowner sitting on a surface lot in LIC currently pays low taxes because the assessed improvement value is minimal. Under LVT, they'd owe taxes based on the land's full potential value — making it fiscally painful to leave it underdeveloped.

**Open question:** What would LVT do to neighborhoods that are *intentionally* low-density (parks, community gardens, single-family areas)? Is there a carve-out mechanism?

## NYC Property Tax Mechanics

Learned while running numbers on 350 E 52nd. Useful reference.

Sources: [NYC DOF — Property Tax Rates](https://www.nyc.gov/site/finance/property/property-tax-rates.page) · [Assessed Value](https://www.nyc.gov/site/finance/property/property-determining-your-assessed-value.page) · [Definitions](https://www.nyc.gov/site/finance/property/definitions-of-property-assessment-terms.page)

| Class | Property type | Assessment ratio | Tax rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1-3 family homes | 6% of market value | 19.843% |
| 2 | Residential 4+ units | 45% of market value | 12.439% |
| 3 | Utilities | 45% of market value | 11.108% |
| 4 | Commercial (incl. parking lots) | 45% of market value | 10.848% |

- Surface parking lots = **Class 4**
- Large residential buildings = **Class 2**
- The "taxable billable AV" is what the tax rate is actually applied to — it's the assessed value after any caps or exemptions
- Note: Class 4 (parking) has a *lower* tax rate than Class 2 (residential), which makes the fiscal gap between a surface lot and a residential building even larger than it first appears

## Induced Demand

Mentioned briefly but not explored. The basic claim: building more road capacity doesn't reduce congestion, it generates new car trips to fill it. Counterintuitive but well-documented.

- The classic study is Duranton & Turner (2011) — "The Fundamental Law of Road Congestion"
- Local application: the BQE, Queens Boulevard history, highway widening proposals that keep coming up
- Related to parking: if you build more parking, you induce more driving to fill it — same logic

## Parking Minimums vs. Maximums

- Most zoning codes set *minimums* — you must provide at least X spaces
- Reform position: switch to *maximums* — you may provide at most X spaces
- NYC actually eliminated parking minimums for most of the city in 2023 (City of Yes for Housing Opportunity) — worth looking into what actually changed and whether it's being applied in CB2 territory
- **Open question:** Has the 2023 NYC parking minimum elimination had any measurable effect on new development applications in Queens?

## Strong Towns Methodology

- Chuck Marohn's framework for evaluating land use fiscally
- Core tool: tax revenue per acre — compare what a parcel generates vs. what it costs the city to service
- Their database and case studies are at strongtowns.org
- Good source to cite when making the fiscal argument at community board — it's not just ideology, it's an accounting framework

## Suburban Sprawl as a Policy Choice: The Carmel Valley Case

Carmel Valley (San Diego) is a textbook example of master-planned sprawl — built entirely around the car, no walkable center, every trip requires driving, retail in strip malls with massive parking lots. Most of northern San Diego follows the same pattern. The question is whether this was inevitable or a policy choice with real costs.

**The policy choice argument:**
- The roads, highways, and interchanges that make it accessible were heavily subsidized by state/federal gas taxes and general funds — not paid for by the people who chose to live far from everything
- Low density means every city service (trash, water lines, emergency response, schools) costs more per household to deliver than in denser areas
- Car dependency is structurally enforced — even people who'd prefer to walk or take transit can't, because the built environment makes it impossible
- The zoning that mandated this pattern was a deliberate decision, not a market outcome

**The honest tension — families with kids:**
This is the hardest counterargument and it deserves a real answer rather than dismissal. A family with 2-3 kids, wanting a yard, space to spread out, good schools, and somewhere to park multiple cars — their preferences are legitimate. Telling them "you should live in an apartment in Queens" isn't a policy, it's a lifestyle preference dressed up as one.

The more honest question is: **does the current system give people a genuine choice, or does it only offer one option at subsidized prices?**

- Suburbs are cheap partly because their true infrastructure costs are socialized across all taxpayers
- Dense walkable neighborhoods in the US are expensive partly because they're artificially scarce — zoning restricts supply
- If both patterns had to compete on a level fiscal playing field, the market might produce more of a spectrum — some families would still choose sprawl, but at its real cost; others might choose missing middle housing they currently can't afford or find

**What the argument isn't:**
- It's not "everyone should live in NYC apartments"
- It's not a moral judgment on the people who live in Carmel Valley (most just bought what was available and affordable)
- It's not even anti-single-family-home — it's anti-*mandated* single-family-only zoning

**The actual reform position:**
Allow the market to build missing middle housing (3-6 story walkups, duplexes, townhouses, small apartment buildings) in places currently zoned exclusively for single-family. Houston has no zoning and didn't become all skyscrapers — it built a lot of mid-density organically. The "not everything can be high-rises" objection defeats a position nobody actually holds, in order to protect the status quo. Worth being able to name that move.

**Open question:** How would you even analyze Carmel Valley fiscally? Tax revenue per acre compared to a mixed-use town center of the same square footage? Infrastructure cost per household vs. a walkable neighborhood? This feels like a Strong Towns-style analysis applied to San Diego suburban data — worth looking for whether anyone has done this for North County SD specifically.

## NYC Property Tax Regressive Structure

Came up as an extension of the tax class discussion. The system looks progressive on the surface (small homeowners pay a "higher" 19.8% rate) but is actually regressive in practice because the assessment ratio makes the effective rate for Class 1 homes far lower than for rental buildings.

**The numbers:**
- $3M Class 1 home: assessed at 6% → $180K AV → tax bill ~$35K/year → **effective rate ~1.2%**
- $3M Class 2 rental building: assessed at 45% → $1.35M AV → tax bill ~$168K/year → **effective rate ~5.6%**
- The landlord doesn't absorb that gap — it passes through to tenants. So renters (who skew lower income) bear a burden that homeowners (who skew higher wealth) largely escape.

**The legitimate counterargument:**
Many longtime homeowners are asset-rich but cash-poor. A retired person who bought in Woodside for $80K in 1975 now owns something worth $1.2M but lives on a fixed income. Taxing them at 5.6% effective rate ($67K/year) would force them to sell. The Class 1 protection is partly a mechanism to let people age in place as values appreciate around them.

**The policy problem:**
The protection is blunt — it covers the cash-poor Woodside retiree *and* the wealthy West Village speculator equally. A smarter system would tie the relief to income, not property class. The NYC Advisory Commission on Property Tax Reform recommended changes in 2021; nothing has passed politically.

**The framing that works vs. the framing that doesn't:**
- Doesn't work at a community board: "single family homes are a sin" / moral framing
- Works: "this pattern was a policy choice, we collectively subsidize it, and we don't have to keep choosing it for *new* development"
- The existing homeowners in the room aren't the target — *future* zoning decisions are
